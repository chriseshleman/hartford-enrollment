What follows is a careful attempt to pull insights from public numbers regarding student enrollment in Hartford, put together without accompanying claim of any expertise in education policy or the history of greater Hartford. Every source is named, every step is written down, and the repository is linked below, so anyone who knows the subject better can check the work and suggest improvements.

Why it matters: this rests on figures assembled for this page, and better numbers would sharpen it. But the pattern is hard to miss. Fewer students per school building usually means fewer teachers, thinner course offerings, and harder choices about how to pay for them to stay open — even in years when citywide enrollment holds roughly steady.

A follow-up question

Where did the students go?

The obvious guess upon seeing the data above is "fewer kids in the city means fewer kids in schools." Hartford's child population did fall. But for the years where the state counts Hartford students by where they actually enroll, something else is happening alongside it — and it moves faster than the population does.

Traditional Hartford-run schools once taught the lion's share of the city's children. By 2024-25 they taught fewer than half.

How this was built

Methodology

Every script, source file and intermediate table lives in the project repository. Corrections and pull requests are welcome, particularly on the reconstructed years.

Chart 1 — average enrollment

1. What it measures. Students attending Hartford district schools, wherever those students live. It includes suburban children in Hartford's inter-district magnets. A "legacy" school is one that clears the two filters in step 4.

2. Sources. Two, joined on twelve-digit federal school ID. The National Center for Education Statistics Common Core of Data Public School Universe Survey covers 1986-87 to 1997-98 via twelve annual fixed-width files. The Urban Institute's Education Data Portal covers years 1998 to 2024.

3. Using the old files. The total-enrollment field moves position between years as the National Center for Education Statistics widened its grade columns — character 243 in 1986-87, 258 by 1988, 263 from 1991. The offsets were taken from the published record layouts and verified: all continuously-reporting Hartford schools matched data from the agency's own ELSI tool. Occasional single-year data gaps are interpolated using moving averages from surrounding years.

4. Choosing the schools. Two filters: at least thirty consecutive years of data, and no single year below 150 students. Twenty-four of Hartford's seventy schools qualify. The 150 floor exists to exclude tiny specialty programs, not schools that shrank — shrinking is the subject of the analysis, so filtering it out would remove the effect being measured.

Chart 2 — Hartford-resident students by school type

1. What it measures. Hartford residents, wherever they attend — a different population from chart one. A Hartford child bussed to a suburban district appears here and not there; a suburban child in a Hartford magnet appears there and not here.

2. Sources. 2016-17 onward is read directly from the Connecticut State Department of Education's EdSight Resident Town export. Traditional enrollment before 2016 is taken from federal counts of Hartford's non-magnet district schools. Magnets and choice, 2002-03 to 2015-16, are derived from the Sheff v. O'Neill compliance rate — the share of Hartford minority students in reduced-isolation settings, which the state reported to the court each year; six anchor years are used, with straight lines between them. Vo-tech numbers are scaled from A. I. Prince Technical High School, in Hartford, which federal data reports continuously from 1986-87.

3. Caveats. The resident total is not measured before 2016. It follows arithmetically from traditional enrollment, vo-tech, and the court-reported share. Where the Sheff record disagrees with itself, the lower figure is used, which understates the shift rather than overstating it — the small dip at 2006-07 is one of those. In 2013-14 the court narrowed its definition of "minority," and several magnets became compliant without a child changing schools, so the rate either side of that line measures slightly different things. Hatched bars also vary in height more than solid ones, because they mix a federal headcount with an interpolated rate; treat that wobble as noise. Before 2002 there is little to reconstruct: charters opened in 1997, Open Choice in 1998, and federal data puts Hartford magnet enrollment at zero for 1999 through 2001. Flat early bars are a finding, not a gap.
